**The 6 Youngest Squads at the 2026 World Cup**

The average squad age across all 48 teams at this World Cup sits at roughly 27.5 years. These six nations come in noticeably below that line — and each one tells a completely different story about why.

Some are mid-rebuild, throwing inexperienced talent into the deep end because the previous generation has moved on. Others have stumbled into a golden generation that happens to have arrived all at once, still in their early twenties. And one of them is the reigning European champion, somehow combining elite quality with one of the youngest rosters in the entire tournament.

Here's the full breakdown, from youngest to sixth-youngest.

---

**1. Ivory Coast — Average Age 25.35**

The youngest squad at the 2026 World Cup belongs to Ivory Coast, and the number reflects a complete generational handover. The Elephants spent over a decade leaning on the golden generation built around Didier Drogba and Yaya Touré. That era is long gone, and coach Emerse Faé has rebuilt almost from scratch.

The new core is startlingly young. Yan Diomandé and Bazoumana Touré are both 20, Christ Inao Oulai is 19 — none of them carry the weight of past expectations, only the freedom to play without it. Around them sits a mid-20s group doing the heavy lifting: Simon Adingra at 24, Amad Diallo at 23, Ange-Yoan Bonny at 22. The only real veterans are Franck Kessié at 29 and Jean Michael Seri at 34, both there to provide a steadying presence rather than carry the team.

Six players in the squad are under 23. Only four are 30 or older. This is a team built for the next decade as much as for this tournament.

**2. Ecuador — Average Age 25.58**

Ecuador's number tells a slightly different story — this isn't really a rebuild, it's a golden generation that happens to still be young. The spine of this squad is genuinely elite: Moisés Caicedo, Willian Pacho and Piero Hincapié are all 24, all playing at major European clubs, and all already established internationals with real tournament experience.

Then there's Kendry Páez — 19 years old and arguably the most hyped teenager at the entire World Cup. If Ecuador make a run in this tournament, expect his name to be everywhere.

The one outlier pulling the average upward is captain Enner Valencia at 36, Ecuador's all-time top scorer and the elder statesman of an otherwise remarkably young group. Seven players in the squad are under 23 — the joint-highest figure of any of these six nations.

---

**3. Bosnia & Herzegovina — Average Age 25.92 (tied)**

Bosnia's youth isn't really a choice — it's closer to a necessity. After a dramatic qualification campaign that saw them beat both Wales and Italy on penalties to reach their second ever World Cup, the squad that emerged is a genuine mix of generations colliding out of circumstance rather than design.

At one end sit teenagers Kerim-Sam Alajbegović and Mladen Jurkas, both just 18. At the other end sits Edin Džeko — at 40, Bosnia's all-time top scorer and almost certainly playing his final major tournament. In between, very little. Six players in the squad are under 23, and four are 30 or older, with not much depth in the middle.

It's an unusual age profile, but it's also exactly the kind of squad that can play with freedom — nothing to lose, a legend in the dressing room, and a point to prove against bigger names.

**4. Morocco — Average Age 25.92 (tied)**

Unlike Bosnia, Morocco's youth is entirely deliberate. After Walid Regragui's departure following Morocco's historic 2022 semifinal run, new coach Mohamed Ouahbi has kept the defensive structure that made the Atlas Lions so hard to beat — while quietly building the next generation around it.

Ayyoub Bouaddi is just 18. Chemsdine Talbi and Samir El Mourabet are both 20. These aren't squad fillers — they're players Ouahbi clearly intends to build the next cycle around, introduced now while there's still a settled system to learn from.

Seven players in the squad are under 23 — tied for the highest of any of these six nations — while six are 30 or older, giving Morocco a genuinely balanced spread rather than a stark generational gap. For a country that just reached its first ever World Cup semifinal, building the next generation in parallel rather than waiting for the current one to retire looks like smart long-term planning.

---

**5. Tunisia — Average Age 26.15**

Tunisia's relatively young average sits alongside one of the most remarkable defensive records in World Cup qualifying history — they reached this tournament without conceding a single goal across ten qualifying matches.

The squad's spine is experienced without being old. Ellyes Skhiri captains from midfield with over 80 caps, and Hannibal Mejbri brings Premier League energy alongside him. At the other end of the age spectrum, 18-year-old Rayan Elloumi is the youngest player in the squad and one to watch as a potential breakout name.

Six players are under 23, while seven are 30 or older — a squad with experience at the back and energy further forward, built around defensive discipline rather than individual stars.

**6. Spain — Average Age 26.19**

This is the statistic that might matter most at the entire tournament. Spain aren't a rebuild, and they aren't a golden generation that happens to be young by accident. They are the reigning European champions, widely considered among the best teams in the world — and they're doing it with one of the youngest squads in the field.

Lamine Yamal is 18. Pau Cubarsí is 19. Both are already first-name-only talents recognised across world football. But the real depth of Spain's youth runs further than the headline names — Gavi is 21, Pedri is 23, Ferran Torres is 26. These aren't promising kids waiting for their chance. They've already played major tournaments and won them.

Five players in the squad are under 23, six are 30 or older — and manager Luis de la Fuente has, in effect, no succession problem. The next generation of Spanish football isn't coming. It's already on the pitch, currently the best team in the world, and still years away from its peak.

Six squads, six different stories — a complete rebuild (Ivory Coast), a golden generation that arrived early (Ecuador), a generational collision born of circumstance (Bosnia), deliberate succession planning (Morocco), defensive steel built around youth (Tunisia), and a champion that somehow gets to have it both ways (Spain).
