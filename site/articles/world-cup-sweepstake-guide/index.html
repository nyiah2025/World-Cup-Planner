How to Do a World Cup Sweepstake — The Complete Guide (+ Free Kit)

Every World Cup, somewhere in an office or a group chat, someone says the words. "Should we do a sweepstake?" And then everyone agrees — and then nobody does anything — and then it's matchday and it's too late. Don't be that group. A well-run sweepstake takes about twenty minutes to set up and pays dividends for seven weeks. It gives people a team to follow, a reason to watch matches they'd otherwise skip, and a running conversation that outlasts the tournament itself.

This guide covers the whole thing: what a sweepstake actually is, how to handle the maths with 48 teams (this is new territory — previous World Cups had 32, which was a much tidier number), how to run a fair draw, and what prize structures actually work for different group sizes. 1. What Is a World Cup Sweepstake?

The format is simple: each participant pays into a pot, draws a team at random, and wins (or loses) based on how far that team gets in the tournament. Your luck in the draw is everything. You might pull Argentina. You might pull Haiti. That's the point. The randomness is what makes it social — it gives the football obsessive and the person who only watches for the vibes exactly the same starting position.

Unlike a prediction league or a fantasy team, there's no ongoing skill component. Once the draw is done, you're a fan of your team whether you like it or not. That's actually what makes sweepstakes survive in offices and friend groups that would never sustain a weekly prediction league — the commitment ends at the draw. The engagement runs the whole tournament.

"The sweepstake is the one format where knowing nothing about football is a genuine competitive advantage. Beginner's luck is real. Plan accordingly."

Why 2026 Is the Best Year to Run One

This is the first World Cup with 48 teams — up from 32 in every previous edition. More teams means more nations represented, more matches to follow, and crucially: more variety in the draw. With 48 teams across 12 groups, the gap between a "good" draw and a "bad" draw is smaller than it used to be. There's no outright guaranteed-to-fail team. Even the tournament's longer shots have genuine upset potential in a 48-team bracket where the Round of 32 is decided match by match.

The downside — and we'll address it fully — is that 48 doesn't divide cleanly into most group sizes. That's the main new problem this tournament creates, and the maths section below solves it properly.

2. Before You Start: Agree the Rules First

The arguments in any sweepstake — and there will be arguments, usually over prize distribution — happen because the rules weren't set in writing before the draw. Fix this by deciding everything in advance and sending it to the group before anyone pays in. The rules don't have to be complex. They just have to exist.

The Four Things to Agree Before the Draw

Pre-Draw Agreement Checklist
  • Entry fee and pot size — How much per person? $5 is the office standard in the UK; $10 is common in the US. Decide whether everyone pays the same or whether multiple entries are allowed at double the fee.
  • What wins — Does the winner take all (team that wins the Final)? Or is prize money split across stages (e.g. reaching the semis, the Final, winning it)? The split-prize model keeps more people engaged longer.
  • How leftover teams are handled — With 48 teams, you will almost certainly have teams left over. Decide in advance: redistribute them, auction them off, or simply leave them unpicked. More on this below.
  • Who organises the payouts — Someone has to hold the money and pay out at the end. Agree who this is before the pot exists. The less ambiguity here, the better.

Multiple Entries: Yes or No?

If your group is small — say, under ten people — allowing multiple entries is a good way to get more teams in play and increase the pot. Someone pays twice, they draw twice. Simple. The risk is that one person ends up with a disproportionate chunk of the good teams, which sours the mood. A sensible cap is two entries per person maximum. Beyond that, the feel of the sweepstake starts to shift from fun communal tradition to one person's personal prediction league.

Send the rules in writing. A WhatsApp message or email before anyone hands over money is all it takes. It doesn't need to be a legal document. It needs to be something people can point at when a dispute arises at the quarter-final stage. And a dispute will arise. It always does.

3. The 48-Team Maths

Here's the new challenge for 2026. With 32 teams — as in every World Cup from 1998 to 2022 — the maths was beautifully clean. Eight people got four teams each. Sixteen people got two teams each. The numbers cooperated.

48 teams is a different beast. It divides neatly by 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, and 24. It does not divide neatly by 5, 7, 9, 10, 11, 13, 14, 15, 17, 18, 19, 20, or most other group sizes you're likely to have. So leftovers are a feature of the 2026 sweepstake, not a bug. Plan for them.

How to Handle the Leftovers

Redistribute (small remainders): If you have 3 or 4 teams left after everyone draws, put those remaining cards back in the hat. Do a second mini-draw — the same people who drew first draw again, in random order, until the leftover cards are gone. Those people end up with one extra team. No additional cost, no fuss.

Auction (medium remainders): If you have 5–9 teams left, run a quick live auction. Start the bidding at 50p / 50 cents per team. The proceeds go into the pot. This is genuinely fun — someone always bids irrationally high for their country, and the drama of the auction becomes part of the event. It also increases the pot, which everyone benefits from.

Second draw (large remainders): If you have 10+ teams remaining, split the group into two halves and run a second full round of draws at half the entry fee. Everyone gets a second team for half price. More teams, more investment, more fun. The second draw teams are treated exactly the same as the first draw in the prize structure.

The sweet spots for 2026: Groups of 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, or 24 get perfectly clean draws with zero leftovers. If your group is close to one of these numbers — say you have 10 people — consider inviting two more, or allowing one person to take a second entry. Getting to 12 makes life significantly easier.

4. How to Run the Draw

The physical draw — everyone in the same room, hand in a hat — is the version worth doing if at all possible. There's something genuinely irreplaceable about watching someone pull a team out of a bowl and the room's reaction telling you immediately whether they've done well. The groan of "Qatar" landing on someone. The barely-concealed excitement of pulling Argentina. These moments don't happen the same way on a Slack channel at 2pm on a Tuesday.

The Physical Draw: Step by Step

Running the Draw — On the Day
  • Print the sweepstake kit and cut out all 48 team cards. This takes about ten minutes — delegate it to someone who arrives early.
  • Fold each card in half (or quarters for a bigger hat) so no team name is visible. The fold must be consistent — you don't want someone who can feel the weight of a heavy flag emoji to game the system.
  • Drop all 48 cards into a bowl, hat, mug, or bag. Mix thoroughly. The more theatrical the mixing, the better.
  • Decide the draw order. Alphabetical by first name is fairest and avoids anyone arguing about who went first. Or use a random number generator if you want to be rigorous.
  • Each person reaches in and draws their card(s) without looking. Unfold publicly. Let the room react before moving on.
  • Record each draw on the record sheet as you go. Don't trust anyone's memory by the end of the draw.
  • Handle any leftover teams immediately using the agreed method (redistribute, auction, or second draw). Don't leave it for later — it never gets resolved later.

The Remote Draw: When Everyone Isn't in the Same Room

Office sweepstakes increasingly run across remote teams. The key is making it feel like a live event rather than an admin task. Use a video call. Have one person manage the physical draw — they print the kit, cut the cards, and draw on behalf of each participant in turn, showing the card to the camera before announcing it. Record the session so anyone who couldn't attend can catch up.

Alternatively, use a wheel-of-names tool or random team generator online, run it on screen share, and do the draw live in the call. Less tactile, but just as fair. Whatever you use, do it live — asynchronous draws where teams are assigned privately and revealed via spreadsheet lose about 90% of the atmosphere.

Avoiding the Common Draw Day Disasters

Two things go wrong most often. First: not having the kit ready before people arrive. Someone agreed to print it, they forgot, now everyone's waiting while someone scrambles to find a printer. Use the kit link in advance and make sure one specific person is responsible for it. Second: the draw order dispute. Someone always thinks they should have gone earlier or later. Agree the order before it starts, write it down, stick to it.

Time it right. The best moment to run the draw is a day or two before the tournament starts — close enough that the excitement is building, but with enough time for people to look up their teams, do some reading, and arrive at the first match with opinions. Running the draw at half-time during the opening game is too late. Running it three weeks before is too early for anyone to care.

5. Prize Structures That Actually Work

Winner-takes-all is the simplest structure and produces the most dramatic final if your team is still in it. But it also means that by the quarter-finals, most of the group has stopped caring — their team is out, their stake is lost, and the sweepstake has gone cold. For a tournament that runs seven weeks, that's a poor return on the setup effort. A tiered structure that pays out at multiple stages keeps everyone invested for longer.

The Recommended Split: Three Tiers

🥇 Winner (50%)
Wins the Final
Largest share goes to whoever drew the World Cup champion. Non-negotiable centrepiece of the prize structure.
🥈 Finalist (25%)
Reaches the Final
The losing finalist gets a consolation prize. Keeps the runner-up invested through to the Final rather than tuning out after the semi.
🥉 Semi-finalists (25%)
Split Between Two
The two losing semi-finalists split the remaining quarter equally. Means four people are still live by the last week of the tournament.
Example with 12 people at $5 each ($60 pot): Winner $30 · Finalist $15 · Each semi-finalist $7.50. With 12 people and 4 teams each, that means 4 people are in prize contention all the way to the last week. This is the ideal format for an office of that size.

Alternative Structures

Winner takes all: Best for very small groups (4–6 people) where everyone has so many teams they're likely to be engaged regardless. Also best when the group just wants a simple, easy-to-explain format and doesn't want to calculate percentages at midnight after the Final.

Stage-by-stage payouts: For larger groups (20+), consider paying small prizes at each knockout stage — something nominal ($2) for every team that reaches the Round of 16, $5 for every team reaching the quarters, and so on. The winner takes the big prize. This structure maximises engagement at every stage but requires more administration. Only worth doing if someone in the group is willing to track it properly.

Last place consolation: The person whose team exits the tournament earliest gets a small consolation prize — the entry fee back, or a novelty trophy. It rewards bad luck, gets a laugh at the draw when someone pulls Qatar or Haiti, and gives every participant a reason to watch the opening group games. Highly recommended for groups of 10 or more.

6. Running It All Tournament Long

The draw is the event. The tournament is the aftermath. What keeps a sweepstake alive from the group stage through to the Final isn't the prize money — it's the running conversation. Here's how to sustain it.

The Group Chat Is the Product

Create a dedicated group chat the moment the draw is done. Post the full draw results there — who has which team, how many teams each person holds. Pin it. Then keep it active with match updates, genuine reactions, and the odd bit of football chat. The people who know nothing about football will start picking up vocabulary within a week simply because they have a stake in the outcome. That's the sweepstake doing its job.

Score Updates Keep Everyone In

Not everyone will watch every match. Someone's teams might be playing at 2am their time, or during a work meeting they couldn't avoid. Whoever's watching sends score updates to the group. This is the glue. The person who draws Brazil and can't watch the match but gets a running commentary from the group chat at midnight — they are more invested than they would be if they'd watched it alone in silence. Create the shared experience even for people who can't be there live.

Track Results Against the Tournament Bracket

Use the World Cup 2026 wallchart to track how the tournament is progressing — all 12 groups through to the Final, so you can see at a glance which teams are still live and which are out. Print one for the office. Fill it in together after each round of results. It's distinct from the sweepstake draw sheet (which records who drew which team) — the wallchart tracks the actual tournament progression, which is what tells you whether your sweepstake team is still alive.

Weekly sweepstake updates. Send a group update at the end of each knockout round showing who's still live and who's out. Keep it short: "After the Round of 16: Sarah (France ✅), Tom (Argentina ✅), Dave (England 😭), Priya (Brazil ✅)..." This takes two minutes and keeps the whole group feeling like participants rather than observers.

The Long Game: Don't Let It Go Quiet

Sweepstakes die in the gap between the group stage and the knockout rounds. The round of group matches takes three weeks, then there's a brief pause before the Round of 32. This is when engagement drops. Counter it proactively: run a separate golden boot side-bet (everyone names a top scorer for a nominal additional stake), or post a "who's left in the sweepstake" update at the start of the knockout phase to remind people their teams are still alive. The aim is to have no week where the group chat goes completely silent about the tournament.

7. FAQ

What if two people in the group are from the same country as one of the teams?
This one comes up every tournament. The fairest approach: if someone draws their home nation, they can either keep it (and accept the conflict of interest is part of the fun) or put it back and redraw. Agree the rule before the draw — not after someone pulls England and their British colleague is already glaring at them.
Can I transfer my team to someone else if I can't follow the tournament?
Only if the organiser agrees and it's declared openly to the group. Private transfers undermine the communal nature of the sweepstake and cause arguments when the transferred team wins. The cleaner rule: once drawn, teams are non-transferable. If someone leaves the company or goes off-grid, their entry is forfeited and their teams go back into the hat.
What happens if a team withdraws from the tournament due to injury, political reasons, or other circumstances?
Rare, but possible. The most pragmatic rule: if a team withdraws before playing a single match, the holder draws a replacement from the remaining undrawn teams. If they've already played at least one match, they keep the team and accept their luck is out. This is the same rule most established sweepstake traditions use.
Should I charge the same entry fee for leftover teams in a second draw?
Half price is the standard. The first draw teams had the full field to pick from — the second draw is picking from the remainder. Half the fee is fair, increases the pot modestly, and doesn't price anyone out of extra engagement. If you're auctioning leftover teams, let the market decide, but set a 50p / 50 cent floor.
Is a sweepstake legal?
In most countries, yes — small private sweepstakes between friends or colleagues that don't involve profit for the organiser are exempt from gambling regulations. The key conditions are typically that it's private (not open to the public), the organiser takes no cut, and there's no commercial element. This is not legal advice — if you're in doubt about the rules in your jurisdiction, check them. But the classic office sweepstake that's been running since the 1966 World Cup has generally been fine.
What's the ideal group size for a 2026 sweepstake?
For the cleanest draw with no leftovers: 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, or 24 people. Of these, 12 is the sweet spot for most office or friend-group sweepstakes — four teams each, a manageable prize pool, and enough teams to generate genuine debate about who drew well. If your group naturally sits at 10 or 15, recruit two more people — it's worth it for the tidier maths.
