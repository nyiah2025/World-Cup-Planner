Sixteen Stadiums, Three Countries, One Tournament

Sixteen stadiums. Three countries. One tournament unlike anything football has seen before. Capacities across these venues range from 45,000 to over 94,000, and together they'll host all 104 matches of the 2026 World Cup — from the opening whistle in Mexico City to the Final at MetLife Stadium on July 19th. If you're still getting up to speed on how the whole tournament works, start with the complete 2026 World Cup format guide.

One quirk worth knowing before we start: FIFA strips sponsor names from every venue during the tournament. The stadiums you know by their corporate names — MetLife, AT&T, SoFi, Lumen — all get neutral "host city" names for World Cup purposes, since the companies that paid for those naming rights aren't official FIFA sponsors. We'll use the names you actually recognise throughout this guide, with FIFA's official tournament name noted for each.

This isn't just a list of capacities. Some of these venues carry serious World Cup history. Others are engineering marvels built specifically to solve the unique challenge of hosting football in North American summer heat. And two of them sit at an altitude that could genuinely shape who wins this tournament. For the complete picture of which matches are staged at each venue and how the bracket path unfolds into the knockouts, see this guide to the Round of 32.

Mexico's Three Venues

Mexico is the only host nation with multiple venues at altitude — a geographical fact that has real competitive implications for this tournament. Two of the three Mexican stadiums sit high enough above sea level that visiting teams have specifically planned acclimatisation camps around them. See The Altitude Factor below for the full breakdown.

🇲🇽
Mexico City, Mexico
Estadio Azteca
Opened 1966 · Altitude: 2,200 m (7,218 ft)
83,000 capacity FIFA name: Mexico City Stadium

There is no more historic venue at this entire World Cup. Opened in 1966, Estadio Azteca becomes the first stadium ever to host matches at three separate World Cups — 1970, 1986, and now 2026. It hosted the opening match of this tournament, just as it did in 1970 and 1986, making it the first venue in history to open three different World Cups.

The history here runs deep. The 1970 Final was played at the Azteca, where Brazil beat Italy 4–1 in what's still regarded by many as the greatest World Cup Final ever. Sixteen years later, the Azteca hosted the 1986 Final too — Argentina's 3–2 win over West Germany. But perhaps the most famous moments in the stadium's history came in the quarterfinal earlier in that same 1986 tournament, when Diego Maradona scored both his infamous "Hand of God" goal and his "Goal of the Century" — a mazy 60-yard solo run past five England players — in the same match, on the same pitch.

Sitting at 2,200 metres above sea level, the Azteca's altitude is also a genuine competitive factor — see The Altitude Factor below for what that means in practice.

🇲🇽
Guadalajara, Mexico
Estadio Akron
Opened 2010 · Altitude: 1,566 m (5,138 ft)
48,000 capacity FIFA name: Guadalajara Stadium

The smallest of Mexico's three venues, Estadio Akron opened in 2010 and is instantly recognisable for its volcano-inspired exterior — a design echoing the volcanic landscape surrounding Guadalajara. It's the home ground of Chivas de Guadalajara, one of Mexico's most popular clubs, and one of the most visually distinctive stadiums in North America.

For the 2026 World Cup, it's the only one of the 16 host venues that won't stage a single knockout match — group stage fixtures only. At 1,566 metres above sea level, it's the second of Mexico's two high-altitude venues. Visiting teams playing here face real acclimatisation challenges, even if the effect is less severe than at the Azteca.

🇲🇽
Monterrey, Mexico
Estadio BBVA
Opened 2015 · Altitude: 540 m (1,772 ft)
53,500 capacity FIFA name: Monterrey Stadium

Nicknamed "The Steel Giant," Estadio BBVA opened in 2015 and offers spectacular views of the surrounding Sierra Madre mountains from its seating bowl. It's the home of CF Monterrey and has quickly established itself as one of the finest modern football venues in North America.

At roughly 540 metres above sea level, Monterrey sits at a far more modest elevation than the other two Mexican venues — altitude simply isn't a factor here the way it is in Mexico City or Guadalajara. The stadium combines aesthetic ambition with a bowl design that creates an excellent atmosphere for big matches.

The Altitude Factor

Here's something most fans outside Mexico have never had to think about: two of the 16 World Cup venues sit high enough above sea level to genuinely affect how football is played.

Every other venue at this tournament — including Estadio BBVA in Monterrey — is low enough that altitude isn't a meaningful factor. But the Azteca and Akron are a different story entirely.

At elevation, the air is thinner. The ball travels differently — generally further and faster, since there's less air resistance. And for players whose bodies aren't acclimatised, fatigue sets in noticeably faster. Sprinting, recovering, pressing — all of it costs more oxygen when the air is thin.

FIFA's altitude rules: Stadiums above 2,500 metres require a minimum acclimatisation period for visiting teams, and that requirement extends to two weeks for venues above 3,000 metres. The Azteca, at 2,200 metres, sits just below that hard threshold — but the effect is still very real. Visiting nations have spent weeks training at altitude specifically to prepare for matches there.

This is widely seen as a genuine home advantage for Mexico. Their deepest World Cup runs have historically come on home soil — the 1986 quarterfinal run included — and Mexican football officials have been candid about viewing the Azteca's altitude as "a very potent setting" for their own team.

Interestingly, South Africa — Mexico's opponent in the 2026 opener — may be less affected than most visiting teams. Johannesburg's Soccer City, which hosted the 2010 World Cup opener, sits at around 1,750 metres itself. A team used to playing at altitude at home arrives at the Azteca with less of a shock to the system than a team flying in from sea level.

Canada's Two Venues

Canada co-hosts the tournament for the first time in their history, and both their venues sit in major coastal cities — far from any altitude concerns. The Canadian climate is also, by North American summer standards, relatively forgiving, particularly in Vancouver where temperatures are moderate and Pacific breezes keep the heat manageable.

🇨🇦
Vancouver, Canada
BC Place
Opened 1983 · Retractable roof added 2011
54,000 capacity FIFA name: Vancouver Stadium

Opened in 1983 and extensively renovated since — including the addition of a retractable roof in 2011 — BC Place has serious pedigree on the world stage. It hosted the opening and closing ceremonies of the 2010 Winter Olympics, and in 2015 it hosted the Women's World Cup Final, where the USA beat Japan 5–2 in front of a sellout crowd.

For 2026, BC Place is the deeper of Canada's two venues — hosting seven matches in total, including five group stage games, a Round of 32 match, and a Round of 16 fixture. That's the furthest any Canadian venue goes into the knockout stages. The retractable roof is a genuine asset: matches can be played rain or shine regardless of Vancouver's famously unpredictable Pacific Northwest weather.

🇨🇦
Toronto, Canada
BMO Field
Opened 2007 · Expanded for 2026
45,000 capacity FIFA name: Toronto Stadium

BMO Field opened in 2007 with a capacity of under 30,000 — by far the smallest stadium in this tournament's history at its original size. A $146 million renovation added roughly 17,000 temporary seats to bring it up to the 45,000 required for World Cup hosting, making it still the smallest venue of the 16.

It's the long-time home of Toronto FC and hosts six matches in 2026, including one Round of 32 game. The smaller capacity means demand is fierce and secondary market prices for matches here are among the highest in the tournament — especially for Canada's own group stage fixtures, which have a once-in-a-generation significance for Canadian football.

The USA's Eleven Venues

Eleven of the 16 stadiums are in the United States, spread across the country from the Pacific Coast to the Atlantic seaboard. Most of them are NFL venues — engineering giants built for American football that have been adapted for the beautiful game, with pitches installed, capacity configurations adjusted, and FIFA's operational standards applied on top of facilities that were already world-class.

Check the full 2026 schedule to see exactly which matches are being played at each venue.

🇺🇸
East Rutherford, New Jersey
MetLife Stadium
Opened 2010 · Cost: ~$1.6 billion
82,500 capacity FIFA name: New York New Jersey Stadium

The stadium that gets the biggest prize of all. Opened in 2010, MetLife is the only stadium in the NFL shared by two teams — the Giants and the Jets — and has hosted some of the biggest events in American sports and entertainment, including Super Bowl XLVIII in 2014. For 2026, it hosts eight matches across the tournament — and the eighth is the World Cup Final on July 19th.

The Final at MetLife will be the centrepiece of the entire tournament. The New York metropolitan area has the largest football (soccer) fanbase in the United States, and an estimated 1.5 billion television viewers worldwide are expected to watch the Final from this venue. The sight of a packed MetLife on July 19th will be one of the defining images of the 2026 World Cup.

🇺🇸
Arlington, Texas (Dallas area)
AT&T Stadium
Opened 2009 · Cost: ~$1.3 billion · Retractable roof
94,000 capacity FIFA name: Dallas Stadium

The largest venue of this entire tournament. Opened in 2009, AT&T Stadium features a retractable roof and one of the largest column-free interior spaces of any building in the world. Its massive suspended video board — 160 feet by 72 feet — was a marvel when it was installed and remains one of the largest in sports. The stadium once hosted an NBA All-Star Game that drew a record attendance of 108,713 people.

For 2026, it hosts nine matches — tied for the most of any venue — including a semifinal. The sheer size of the bowl, the retractable roof offering protection from Texas summer heat, and the full air conditioning system make this one of the best-equipped venues at the tournament for high-stakes knockout football. See the full schedule for which matches land in Dallas.

🇺🇸
Atlanta, Georgia
Mercedes-Benz Stadium
Opened 2017 · Cost: ~$1.6 billion · Fully climate-controlled
75,000 capacity FIFA name: Atlanta Stadium

Opened in 2017, Mercedes-Benz Stadium is one of the most distinctive buildings in world sport. Its eight-panel retractable roof — inspired by the oculus of Rome's Pantheon — opens and closes like a camera aperture in under 20 minutes. The stadium also features a 360-degree circular video board known as the "halo board," measuring 58 feet tall and over 1,100 feet around.

It hosted Super Bowl LIII in 2019 and is one of the few fully climate-controlled venues at this World Cup — a genuine advantage given Atlanta's notoriously hot, humid summers. It hosts nine matches in 2026, tied with Dallas for the most of any venue, including a semifinal. For daytime kickoffs in July, the ability to seal the roof and run full air conditioning is a significant competitive and spectator comfort advantage.

🇺🇸
Kansas City, Missouri
Arrowhead Stadium
Opened 1972 · Guinness World Record holder
76,416 capacity FIFA name: Kansas City Stadium

Home of the NFL's Kansas City Chiefs since it opened in 1972, Arrowhead holds the current Guinness World Record for the loudest crowd noise ever recorded at an outdoor stadium — 142.2 decibels, set in 2014 during a Chiefs game, breaking a record Seattle's Lumen Field had set the year before. For context, that's louder than a jet engine at close range — noise above 140 decibels causes immediate hearing damage.

Arrowhead hosts six matches in 2026, including a Round of 16 match and a quarterfinal. The open-air bowl that makes Arrowhead so legendarily loud will funnel crowd noise back onto the pitch for some of the tournament's most important knockout moments. Kansas City has also shown an enthusiastic embrace of football — it hosted a 2026 opening group stage match to enormous crowds.

🇺🇸
Houston, Texas
NRG Stadium
Opened 2002 · Retractable roof · Full air conditioning
72,000 capacity FIFA name: Houston Stadium

Opened in 2002 with a retractable roof, NRG Stadium is one of the most climate-controlled venues in the entire tournament — a serious asset in Houston's brutal summer heat and humidity. It hosted Super Bowl LI in 2017, the game famous for the New England Patriots' historic comeback from a 28–3 deficit in the fourth quarter.

For 2026, NRG hosts several high-profile group stage matches, including fixtures involving Germany. Houston is the most ethnically diverse city in the United States, with one of the largest Latin American communities in the country — meaning World Cup fever in the city runs exceptionally deep. The sealed, air-conditioned environment makes it one of the most comfortable venues to watch football in the July heat.

🇺🇸
Santa Clara, California (San Francisco Bay Area)
Levi's Stadium
Opened 2014 · Home of the San Francisco 49ers
71,000 capacity FIFA name: San Francisco Bay Area Stadium

Opened in 2014 in the heart of Silicon Valley, Levi's Stadium is the home of the San Francisco 49ers and hosted Super Bowl 50 in 2016. It's one of the more technologically advanced venues at this World Cup, reflecting its location at the epicentre of the global tech industry — from its solar panel array to its sophisticated digital infrastructure for fans.

The Bay Area's mild, Mediterranean climate means heat is rarely a serious concern here — temperatures in Santa Clara in June and July are typically moderate, making it one of the more comfortable open-air venues at this tournament. The stadium sits within easy reach of San Francisco by public transit, giving it one of the best-connected locations in the tournament.

🇺🇸
Inglewood, California (Los Angeles area)
SoFi Stadium
Opened 2020 · Translucent canopy roof
70,000 capacity FIFA name: Los Angeles Stadium

Opened in 2020, SoFi Stadium is widely regarded as the most expensive stadium ever built — estimates put the cost at around $5 billion. It's the shared home of the LA Rams and LA Chargers and hosted Super Bowl LVI in 2022. Its signature feature is a translucent canopy roof that shields fans from sun while allowing natural light and air through — distinct from the fully enclosed roofs at Dallas and Atlanta.

The canopy provides shade and some protection from the elements without fully enclosing the venue, offering natural ventilation rather than air conditioning. For 2026, SoFi hosts several major knockout matches. Los Angeles is the second-largest city in the United States and home to the largest Mexican-American community in the country — expect extraordinary atmospheres when Mexico-connected nations play here.

🇺🇸
Philadelphia, Pennsylvania
Lincoln Financial Field
Opened 2003 · Home of the Philadelphia Eagles
69,000 capacity FIFA name: Philadelphia Stadium

Opened in 2003, Lincoln Financial Field is the home of the Philadelphia Eagles and sits within the tournament's busy East Coast cluster of venues. Philadelphia has one of the most passionate sports fanbases in the United States, and that reputation for intensity — the city's fans are among the most famously vocal in American sport — will carry over into the World Cup atmosphere here.

The stadium is open-air, which means summer heat and humidity are a factor. Philadelphia in July can be warm and muggy, particularly for afternoon kickoffs. For players coming from cooler climates, it's one of the more physically demanding environments at the tournament. The stadium hosts a solid slate of group stage and knockout matches as part of the Northeast corridor.

🇺🇸
Seattle, Washington
Lumen Field
Opened 2002 · Cost: ~$430 million · Engineered to be loud
69,000 capacity FIFA name: Seattle Stadium

Opened in 2002, Lumen Field was deliberately engineered to be loud — its clamshell roof design traps and reflects crowd noise back onto the field. In 2013, it set the Guinness World Record for loudest crowd noise at an outdoor stadium, reaching 137.6 decibels during a Seahawks game — a record later broken by Kansas City's Arrowhead. The roof covers around 70% of seats, meaning most fans stay dry regardless of Seattle's famously unpredictable weather.

Lumen Field is also the home ground of Seattle Sounders FC, one of MLS's most successful clubs, meaning this stadium already has a football-specific atmosphere that few other NFL venues can match. It hosts six matches in 2026. Seattle's Pacific Northwest climate is the most reliably mild of any US host city — heat is almost never an issue here.

🇺🇸
Foxborough, Massachusetts (Boston area)
Gillette Stadium
Opened 2002 · Home of the New England Patriots
68,756 capacity FIFA name: Boston Stadium

Opened in 2002, Gillette Stadium is the home of the New England Patriots — one of the most successful franchises in NFL history. It sits in Foxborough, about 30 miles southwest of Boston, and hosts several group stage matches as part of the tournament's Northeast cluster. The Boston area has one of the most concentrated pools of college-educated football (soccer) fans in the United States.

Like the other Northeast venues, Gillette is open-air, and July temperatures in Massachusetts can be warm and humid. The stadium's most distinctive visual feature is the lighthouse-style towers at its north end — an architectural nod to New England's maritime heritage. It's surrounded by extensive parking and entertainment facilities that will serve as a lively pre-match hub for fans.

🇺🇸
Miami Gardens, Florida
Hard Rock Stadium
Opened 1987 · More Super Bowls than any other venue
65,326 capacity FIFA name: Miami Stadium

Opened in 1987, Hard Rock Stadium is the home of the Miami Dolphins and has hosted more Super Bowls than any other venue in NFL history. Miami is also one of the most football-mad cities in the United States — with a huge Caribbean, Central American, and South American diaspora, the appetite for the World Cup here is unlike anywhere else in the country.

For 2026, it hosts the third-place playoff alongside several high-profile group stage matches — fitting for a stadium so accustomed to hosting the biggest occasions in American sport. The open-air bowl and South Florida's notorious summer humidity make this one of the most physically challenging venues for players — temperatures and heat indices in Miami in July are extreme, and there's no relief from a roof. If your team is playing a midday match here, both the players and the crowd are in for a demanding afternoon.

Roofs, Domes and Summer Heat

A World Cup played across North America in June and July brings a challenge most previous tournaments never had to fully confront: serious heat. Temperatures in cities like Dallas, Houston, Atlanta and Monterrey regularly climb into the mid-30s Celsius during the tournament window, and FIFA has been candid that covered, climate-controlled stadiums will be prioritised for daytime kickoffs wherever possible.

Why this matters for your team: A midday kickoff in Atlanta or Houston means comfortable conditions under a sealed, air-conditioned roof. A midday kickoff in Miami or Philadelphia means players and fans baking in open-air conditions. The scheduling implications are significant — teams from cooler climates may find summer heat in the South and Southeast a genuine physical challenge.

Here's how the 16 venues break down across the climate-control spectrum:

❄️
Tier 1 — Full Climate Control
Retractable roof + full air conditioning
AT&T Stadium (Dallas), Mercedes-Benz Stadium (Atlanta), NRG Stadium (Houston). The gold standard. A sealed roof and full AC can maintain comfortable conditions regardless of outdoor temperature. Daytime kickoffs here are manageable for players from any climate.
⛅
Tier 2 — Roof without full AC
Retractable roof, shade, or canopy — no full air conditioning
BC Place (Vancouver), BMO Field (Toronto), SoFi Stadium (Los Angeles). Shade and roof coverage reduce sun exposure but don't actively cool the air. Still a significant improvement over open-air in extreme heat.
🌤️
Tier 3 — Partial coverage
Partial roof or covered sections — significant open-air exposure
Lumen Field (Seattle), Arrowhead Stadium (Kansas City). Partial roof coverage provides some relief, but a significant portion of the bowl remains exposed. Seattle's mild climate means heat is rarely an issue there regardless; Kansas City can get very hot in July.
☀️
Tier 4 — Open-air
No meaningful roof coverage
MetLife Stadium (NJ), Lincoln Financial Field (Philadelphia), Gillette Stadium (Boston), Hard Rock Stadium (Miami), Levi's Stadium (Bay Area), Estadio Azteca (Mexico City), Estadio Akron (Guadalajara), Estadio BBVA (Monterrey). Fully exposed to outdoor conditions. Miami is the most demanding — open-air in South Florida's summer humidity creates extreme heat index conditions for both players and fans.

For fans travelling to the tournament, climate control tier is as important a factor as the quality of the matchup. Check the full schedule and look up your team's kickoff times — a night match in Miami is a very different experience from a midday kickoff in the same city.

The Final Word

Sixteen stadiums, sixteen stories, one tournament. From the most historic ground in World Cup history at the Azteca — host to Pelé's greatest triumph and Maradona's most infamous afternoon — to the loudest crowds in American sport at Arrowhead and Lumen Field, every venue on this list has a part to play in how 2026 unfolds.

Add in the genuine competitive wrinkle of Mexico City's altitude, the engineering response to North American summer heat at Dallas and Atlanta, and the architectural ambition of everything from SoFi's $5 billion canopy to the Azteca's six decades of World Cup history, and this is a tournament where the venues themselves are as much a part of the story as the teams playing in them.

The 2026 World Cup is the first to span three countries and two time zones of competition. The 16 stadiums that host it are, collectively, a cross-section of modern sport — NFL megastructures, historic football cathedrals, and purpose-built modern arenas that between them have hosted Super Bowls, NBA records, World Cup Finals, and some of the greatest moments in the history of the game. Whatever happens on the pitch, the backdrop will be extraordinary.
