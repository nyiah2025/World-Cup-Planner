World Cup Viewing Party Food Guide — A Dish for Every Team

Forty-eight nations. One tournament. And an enormous opportunity to turn match day into a genuine food experience. This guide gives you something for every team competing in 2026 — one featured dish per group with a simple recipe you can actually make, plus a quick spotlight on the other three nations in that group so your spread can match whoever's playing.

Whether you're hosting a full viewing party (check out our Viewing Party Guide for the rest of the setup) or just want to eat something appropriate while your team plays, this is your reference. Pick your team, find their group, and you'll know exactly what to put on the table.

How to use this guide: Jump straight to your team's group using the table of contents above. Each group has one fully developed featured recipe — something you can genuinely cook at home — plus spotlight dishes for the other three nations. Don't forget to check when your team kicks off so you know exactly when to have the food ready.

Group A — Mexico, South Korea, South Africa, Czechia

Group A Teams
Mexico · South Korea · South Africa · Czechia

Mexico's tournament opener makes this an obvious starting point — and also gives us the most natural "make this" tie-in of the entire guide.

⭐ Featured Dish: Tacos al Pastor (Mexico)

Tacos al pastor are Mexico City's signature street food — marinated pork traditionally cooked on a vertical spit, a technique brought to Mexico by Lebanese immigrants in the early 20th century and transformed into something entirely its own. The result is smoky, slightly sweet, deeply savory pork that's impossible to stop eating.

For a home version: marinate thin-cut pork shoulder for at least two hours in a blend of achiote paste, orange juice, white vinegar, garlic, cumin, and dried chili (guajillo or ancho work well). Sear the marinated pork in a hot skillet or on a grill until the edges char slightly. Warm corn tortillas, then build each taco with the pork, a small spoonful of diced pineapple, chopped white onion, fresh cilantro, and a squeeze of lime. Serve with a simple salsa verde on the side.

This is the dish to have ready for Mexico's opening match — and honestly, for any match where you want something that disappears fast.

Make-ahead tip: The pork marinade can be prepared the night before — longer marinating only improves it. Cook in batches and keep warm in a low oven, covered. Set out the toppings in small bowls so guests can build their own tacos through the match.

Spotlight: Korean Fried Chicken (South Korea)

Korean fried chicken — double-fried for an extra-crispy shell, then tossed in a sticky-sweet-spicy glaze of gochujang, soy sauce, garlic, and honey — has become a global phenomenon for good reason. It's genuinely one of the best foods to eat while watching sport: hands-on, shareable, and the contrast between the crunch and the glaze never gets old. For a viewing party, double-fry wings or drumettes, toss them in the sauce while still hot, and finish with sesame seeds and sliced scallion.

Spotlight: Bunny Chow (South Africa)

Bunny chow is one of South Africa's great street food inventions — a hollowed-out loaf of bread filled with curry, originating from the Indian community in Durban. For a party version, hollow out small bread rolls, fill each with a rich, slow-cooked curry (chicken, lamb, or bean-based for a vegetarian option), and let guests eat the bread along with the filling. It's hearty, mess-contained, and a genuine conversation starter.

Spotlight: Smažený Sýr (Czechia)

Smažený sýr — fried cheese — is a beer hall classic across Czechia, and it's exactly what it sounds like: a thick slice of cheese (usually Edam or a similar semi-hard cheese), breaded and deep-fried until the outside is crisp and the inside is molten. Serve it sliced into wedges with tartar sauce and a side of fries. Simple, indulgent, and pairs perfectly with a cold pilsner.

Group B — Canada, Switzerland, Qatar, Bosnia & Herzegovina

Group B Teams
Canada · Switzerland · Qatar · Bosnia & Herzegovina

As one of the three co-hosts and making their World Cup return after 40 years, Canada gets the featured spot here.

⭐ Featured Dish: Poutine (Canada)

Poutine — fries, gravy, and cheese curds — originated in rural Quebec in the 1950s and has since become Canada's unofficial national dish. It's the platonic ideal of comfort food: crispy-on-the-outside fries, drenched in rich brown gravy, studded with squeaky cheese curds that soften but don't fully melt.

For a home version: bake or fry your favorite frozen fries until properly crisp — this matters, since soggy fries make for soggy poutine. While they cook, make a simple gravy by whisking beef or chicken stock into a butter-flour roux, seasoned with a splash of Worcestershire sauce and black pepper. The moment the fries come out of the oven, pile them onto a plate, scatter generously with cheese curds (fresh mozzarella torn into chunks works in a pinch, though it won't squeak), and pour the hot gravy over the top immediately so the cheese just begins to soften.

Make this for any Canada match — it's the easiest, most crowd-pleasing dish in this entire guide.

Spotlight: Cheese Fondue (Switzerland)

Fondue is Switzerland's great communal dish — a pot of melted cheese (traditionally a blend of Gruyère and Emmental) flavored with white wine and a touch of garlic, kept warm at the table while everyone dips in cubes of crusty bread. For a viewing party, this doubles as both food and entertainment — set up a fondue pot or even a slow cooker on low, and let guests take turns dipping. Add some boiled potatoes and apple slices alongside the bread for variety.

Spotlight: Machboos (Qatar)

Machboos is the Gulf region's answer to biryani — fragrant basmati rice slow-cooked with meat (chicken or lamb), warming spices like cinnamon, cardamom, and dried lime, and often finished with a scattering of toasted nuts and raisins. It's a one-pot dish that feeds a crowd easily and fills the house with an incredible aroma. Serve it straight from the pot with a simple side salad and yogurt.

Spotlight: Ćevapi (Bosnia & Herzegovina)

Ćevapi are small grilled sausages made from minced beef and lamb, served in soft flatbread (somun) with raw onion and a creamy, slightly tangy dairy spread called kajmak. They're the centerpiece of Balkan barbecue culture — grill a batch, stuff them into warm pita or flatbread with chopped onion, and serve with a side of ajvar (roasted red pepper relish) if you can find it.

Group C — Brazil, Morocco, Scotland, Haiti

Group C Teams
Brazil · Morocco · Scotland · Haiti

Brazil's opener against Morocco is one of the most anticipated matches of the group stage — and Brazilian party food is some of the easiest and most universally loved on this entire list.

⭐ Featured Dish: Pão de Queijo (Brazil)

Pão de queijo — Brazilian cheese bread — are small, chewy, gooey rolls made from tapioca flour and cheese, naturally gluten-free, and dangerously easy to eat by the dozen. They're a staple at Brazilian gatherings of every kind, served warm straight from the oven.

To make them: blend tapioca starch (also sold as tapioca flour), milk, oil, eggs, salt, and grated Parmesan or a similar hard cheese in a blender until smooth — the batter will be much thinner than a typical bread dough, almost like a thick pancake batter. Pour into greased mini muffin tins and bake at a high temperature (around 200°C/400°F) for 15–20 minutes until puffed and golden. They're best served immediately, while the inside is still molten and stretchy.

This is the dish for Brazil vs Morocco — easy to make in large batches, and they'll be gone before half time.

Spotlight: Briouats (Morocco)

Briouats are small triangular pastries made from thin warqa dough (similar to phyllo), filled with a savory mixture — often spiced ground meat, or a combination of vermicelli and seafood — then folded and fried until golden and crisp. They're a staple of Moroccan gatherings and Ramadan tables alike. For a party, make a batch with a simple spiced beef and onion filling, fold into triangles, and fry until deeply golden. Serve with a side of harissa for heat.

Spotlight: Scotch Pie (Scotland)

The Scotch pie is a small, double-crust meat pie filled with minced mutton or beef, traditionally eaten at football matches across Scotland — which makes it about as appropriate as it gets for this guide. The pastry is firm enough to hold in one hand, making it ideal stadium and viewing-party food alike. Individual pies can be bought ready-made from most bakeries, or made at home with a simple hot-water-crust pastry and well-seasoned minced meat filling, baked until the pastry is crisp and golden.

Spotlight: Marinades (Haiti)

Marinades are Haiti's beloved fried fritters — a savory batter (often flavored with herbs, garlic, and scotch bonnet pepper) studded with bits of vegetable or seafood, deep-fried until crisp and golden. They're a street food staple across Haiti, sold hot from roadside stands. For a party version, mix a simple batter with flour, baking powder, minced herbs, garlic, and finely chopped vegetables or shrimp, then fry spoonfuls until golden. Serve with pikliz — Haiti's fiery pickled cabbage and scotch bonnet relish — on the side for genuine heat.

Group D — USA, Paraguay, Australia, Türkiye

Group D Teams
USA · Paraguay · Australia · Türkiye

As tournament co-hosts, the USA gets the featured spot — and there's no more universal World Cup viewing food than this.

⭐ Featured Dish: Buffalo Wings (USA)

Buffalo wings were invented at the Anchor Bar in Buffalo, New York in 1964, and have since become the single most iconic American sports-watching food. The combination of crispy fried (or baked) chicken wings tossed in a tangy, buttery hot sauce, served alongside cooling blue cheese or ranch dip and crunchy celery, is genuinely hard to beat.

For a home version: pat chicken wings completely dry, then bake at a high temperature (220°C/425°F) on a wire rack for 40–45 minutes, flipping halfway, until the skin is deeply crisp — this gives you the crunch of frying without the mess. While the wings bake, melt butter and whisk in your favorite hot sauce (classic Buffalo wing sauce uses a cayenne-based hot sauce like Frank's). Toss the hot wings in the sauce immediately after they come out of the oven, and serve with celery sticks and a blue cheese or ranch dip.

This is the default dish for any USA match — and honestly works for any match at all.

Spotlight: Chipa (Paraguay)

Chipa is Paraguay's beloved cheese bread — small, dense, slightly chewy rolls made from cassava starch and cheese, similar in spirit to Brazil's pão de queijo but with their own distinct texture and flavor, often including anise seed for a subtle aromatic note. They're traditionally baked in a clay oven but work perfectly well in a regular one. Mix cassava starch, cheese, eggs, milk, and a pinch of anise seed into a dough, shape into small rings or balls, and bake until golden.

Spotlight: Aussie Meat Pies (Australia)

The meat pie is as central to Australian sporting culture as the Buffalo wing is to American — a hand-sized pie filled with minced beef in rich gravy, encased in flaky pastry, traditionally eaten with a squeeze of tomato sauce (ketchup) on top. They're sold at every Australian sporting venue imaginable. For a party, individual frozen meat pies bake easily in batches and taste just as good as anything from a stadium kiosk — serve hot with tomato sauce on the side.

Spotlight: Köfte (Türkiye)

Köfte are Turkish meatballs or meat patties, made from a mixture of minced lamb or beef with onion, garlic, cumin, and parsley, traditionally grilled on skewers over charcoal. The result is smoky, juicy, and deeply savory. For a party, shape the seasoned meat mixture onto skewers or into small patties, grill or pan-fry until charred on the outside, and serve with warm flatbread, a yogurt-based sauce, and a simple tomato and onion salad.

Group E — Germany, Côte d'Ivoire, Curaçao, Ecuador

Group E Teams
Germany · Côte d'Ivoire · Curaçao · Ecuador

Germany's clean group stage draw makes this an easy one to plan for — and German party food is some of the most straightforward on this list.

⭐ Featured Dish: Currywurst (Germany)

Currywurst is Berlin's most famous street food — a grilled or pan-fried bratwurst, sliced into bite-sized pieces, topped with a tangy tomato-based sauce seasoned generously with curry powder. It was invented in West Berlin in 1949 and remains a staple at food stalls across the country to this day.

For a home version: pan-fry or grill bratwurst sausages until well-browned, then slice into bite-sized pieces. For the sauce, combine ketchup with a generous amount of curry powder, a dash of Worcestershire sauce, and a pinch of paprika, warming it gently in a small saucepan. Pour the sauce over the sliced sausage and dust with extra curry powder. Serve with a side of fries (pommes) — the classic pairing.

This is the dish for any Germany match — simple, fast, and genuinely delicious.

Spotlight: Alloco (Côte d'Ivoire)

Alloco is ripe plantain, sliced and deep-fried until caramelized and golden, often seasoned simply with salt or served alongside a spicy onion and tomato sauce. It's a beloved West African street food — sweet, savory, and satisfying all at once. For a party, slice ripe (yellow, slightly black-spotted) plantains diagonally, fry in hot oil until deeply golden on both sides, and serve hot with a simple spicy tomato-onion relish for dipping.

Spotlight: Keshi Yena (Curaçao)

Keshi yena is Curaçao's signature dish — a whole rind of cheese (traditionally Edam or Gouda) stuffed with a savory filling of seasoned shredded chicken, raisins, olives, and peppers, then baked until the cheese melts around the filling. It's a celebratory dish with roots in the island's colonial history. For a party-friendly version, hollow out a large round of Edam or Gouda, fill with the seasoned chicken mixture, top with the reserved cheese, and bake until everything is hot and melted together.

Spotlight: Ceviche (Ecuador)

Ecuadorian ceviche stands apart from other Latin American versions — it's typically made with a tomato-based broth rather than just citrus, giving it a slightly soupier, more savory character, and is often served with shrimp. The fish or shrimp is "cooked" by the acidity of lime juice, combined with diced tomato, onion, cilantro, and a touch of orange juice for sweetness. Serve chilled in small cups or bowls with plantain chips or popcorn on the side — both are traditional Ecuadorian accompaniments.

Group F — Netherlands, Japan, Sweden, Tunisia

Group F Teams
Netherlands · Japan · Sweden · Tunisia

This is one of the most unpredictable groups in the tournament, and Japan's food culture gives us a genuinely fun featured dish.

⭐ Featured Dish: Karaage (Japan)

Karaage is Japanese fried chicken — bite-sized pieces of chicken thigh marinated in soy sauce, ginger, and garlic, coated in a light potato starch dredge, and double-fried until intensely crispy on the outside while staying juicy inside. It's a staple of Japanese izakayas (casual pubs) and one of the most popular foods at any Japanese gathering.

For a home version: cut boneless chicken thigh into bite-sized pieces and marinate for at least 30 minutes in a mixture of soy sauce, grated ginger, grated garlic, and a touch of sake or rice wine. Drain the chicken, toss it in potato starch (cornstarch works as a substitute) until well coated, and fry in batches at a moderate temperature until golden — then fry a second time briefly at a higher temperature for extra crispness. Serve with lemon wedges and Japanese mayo for dipping.

This is the dish for any Japan match — and one of the genuine highlights of this entire guide.

Spotlight: Bitterballen (Netherlands)

Bitterballen are the quintessential Dutch bar snack — small, breaded, deep-fried balls filled with a thick, savory ragout, usually beef, that becomes almost creamy when hot. They're traditionally served with mustard for dipping. For a party, a homemade beef ragout (flour, stock, and finely chopped beef, cooked until thick and paste-like) can be chilled, formed into balls, breaded, and fried — or frozen versions are widely available and work just as well for a crowd.

Spotlight: Swedish Meatballs / Köttbullar (Sweden)

Köttbullar — Swedish meatballs — are made from a mixture of ground beef and pork, bound with breadcrumbs soaked in milk and seasoned with allspice and white pepper, then pan-fried and served in a rich cream gravy. They're traditionally served with lingonberry jam, whose tartness cuts through the richness of the gravy beautifully. For a party, serve the meatballs and gravy in a slow cooker to keep warm, with toothpicks for easy serving, and a small bowl of lingonberry or cranberry sauce alongside.

Spotlight: Brik (Tunisia)

Brik is a Tunisian specialty — a thin sheet of malsouka pastry (similar to phyllo) folded around a filling that traditionally includes a whole egg, tuna or minced meat, capers, and parsley, then fried until the pastry is crisp and the egg inside is just set. The first bite requires care — the egg yolk should still be soft. For a party, prepare individual portions with the filling and a cracked egg, fold carefully, and fry just until golden — best served immediately while the egg is still soft inside.

Group G — Belgium, Egypt, Iran, New Zealand

Group G Teams
Belgium · Egypt · Iran · New Zealand

Belgium's golden generation gets one final World Cup, and Belgian food culture gives us one of the most genuinely fun featured dishes in this guide.

⭐ Featured Dish: Belgian Frites (Belgium)

Belgium claims to have invented the french fry, and regardless of where the truth lies, Belgian frites are a different experience entirely from typical fries — twice-fried for a uniquely crisp exterior and fluffy interior, traditionally served in a paper cone with a generous dollop of mayonnaise or one of dozens of specialty sauces.

For a home version: cut potatoes into thick batons and soak in cold water for at least 30 minutes to remove excess starch, then dry thoroughly. Fry once at a lower temperature (around 150°C/300°F) until soft but pale, then let them rest. Just before serving, fry a second time at a higher temperature (around 190°C/375°F) until deeply golden and crisp. Serve in paper cones if you want to commit to the experience, with a selection of dipping sauces — classic mayonnaise, andalouse sauce (mayo with tomato and peppers), or samurai sauce (a spicy mayo) if you can find or make them.

This is the dish for any Belgium match — simple in concept, but the technique makes all the difference.

Spotlight: Ta'ameya (Egypt)

Ta'ameya is the Egyptian version of falafel, made from dried fava beans rather than chickpeas, giving it a deeper, earthier flavor and a distinctive green-tinged interior from fresh herbs blended into the mixture. The fava beans are soaked, blended with herbs, garlic, and spices, formed into small patties, and deep-fried until crisp. Serve in warm pita with tahini sauce, pickled vegetables, and a simple tomato salad.

Spotlight: Kotlet (Iran)

Kotlet are Persian-style patties made from a mixture of ground meat (usually beef or chicken), mashed potato, grated onion, and egg, seasoned with turmeric and pan-fried until golden on both sides. They're a beloved everyday dish across Iran, often packed for picnics or served at gatherings. Mix the ingredients into a smooth paste, shape into flat oval patties, and pan-fry in a little oil until golden and crisp on both sides. Serve with flatbread, fresh herbs, and pickled vegetables.

Spotlight: Cheese Rolls (New Zealand)

A genuine Southland, New Zealand classic — soft white bread spread with a mixture of grated cheese, onion, and condensed soup (traditionally a creamy onion or mushroom soup mix), rolled up, and grilled or baked until the bread is crisp and the filling is molten. They sound unusual but are an absolute comfort food staple in the south of the country. Spread bread slices with the cheese-and-soup mixture, roll tightly, and grill under a broiler until golden and bubbling.

Group H — Spain, Uruguay, Saudi Arabia, Cape Verde

Group H Teams
Spain · Uruguay · Saudi Arabia · Cape Verde

Spain as European champions makes them the natural feature here, and Spanish tapas culture is built for exactly this kind of viewing.

⭐ Featured Dish: Patatas Bravas (Spain)

Patatas bravas — "fierce potatoes" — are a tapas bar staple across Spain: crispy fried potato cubes served with a spicy tomato-based sauce (the "brava" sauce) and often a drizzle of garlic aioli on top as well. The combination of crunchy potato, smoky-spicy sauce, and creamy aioli is genuinely addictive.

For a home version: cut potatoes into bite-sized cubes and fry (or roast at high heat) until crisp and golden on the outside, tender inside. For the brava sauce, combine tomato paste, smoked paprika, a pinch of cayenne, garlic, and a splash of sherry vinegar, simmered until thickened. For the aioli, whisk together mayonnaise, crushed garlic, and a squeeze of lemon. Drizzle both sauces generously over the hot potatoes and serve immediately with toothpicks or small forks.

This is the dish for any Spain match — and works beautifully alongside other tapas if you want to expand the spread.

Expand the tapas spread: Patatas bravas are the anchor, but add pan con tomate (bread rubbed with tomato and olive oil), a plate of jamón ibérico, and some marinated olives and you've built a proper Spanish viewing table for almost no extra effort.

Spotlight: Chivito (Uruguay)

The chivito is Uruguay's national sandwich — an enormous construction layered with steak, ham, cheese, bacon, egg, lettuce, tomato, and olives, all stacked between bread and often topped with mayonnaise. It's less a sandwich than an event. For a party, prepare the components separately (grilled thin steak, fried bacon, fried eggs, cheese) and let guests build their own — it's messy, enormous, and exactly the kind of food that suits a long match.

Spotlight: Sambousek (Saudi Arabia)

Sambousek are small fried or baked pastries filled with seasoned meat, cheese, or vegetables, folded into triangles or half-moons and crisped until golden. They're popular across the Gulf region as a snack or appetizer, often served during gatherings and celebrations. Fill thin pastry sheets with a simple spiced ground beef and onion mixture, fold into triangles, seal the edges, and fry or bake until golden.

Spotlight: Cachupa (Cape Verde)

Cachupa is Cape Verde's national dish — a slow-cooked stew of corn, beans, and whatever meat or fish is available, often left to simmer for hours until everything melds into a rich, hearty one-pot meal. It's deeply tied to Cape Verdean identity and is traditionally made in large batches for sharing. For a party, a slow cooker works beautifully — combine hominy corn, beans, and your choice of meat (chorizo and pork work well) with onion, garlic, and bay leaf, and let it simmer for several hours until thick and stew-like.

Group I — France, Senegal, Norway, Iraq

Group I Teams
France · Senegal · Norway · Iraq

France landed in the toughest group of the tournament, and French café culture gives us a featured dish that's perfect for grazing through a tense match.

⭐ Featured Dish: Croque Monsieur (France)

The croque monsieur is France's answer to the grilled cheese sandwich — but elevated. Ham and Gruyère cheese between slices of bread, coated in béchamel sauce, then grilled or baked until golden and bubbling. It's a café classic across France, simple but genuinely satisfying.

For a home version: make a quick béchamel by melting butter, whisking in flour to form a paste, then gradually adding milk while whisking until thickened, seasoned with a pinch of nutmeg and salt. Layer ham and grated Gruyère between slices of good bread, spread béchamel over the top of each sandwich, and bake or grill under a broiler until the cheese is melted and golden. For a croque madame variation, top each sandwich with a fried egg.

This is the dish for any France match — and easy to make in batches for a crowd.

Spotlight: Pastels (Senegal)

Pastels are Senegalese fried pastries, similar in spirit to empanadas, filled with a mixture of seasoned fish or meat, onion, and herbs, folded into half-moons and deep-fried until golden. They're a popular street food and party snack across Senegal. Fill small rounds of dough with a spiced fish or beef filling, fold and seal, and fry until crisp and golden — serve with a squeeze of lime and a side of spicy pepper sauce.

Spotlight: Vafler (Norway)

Norwegian waffles are heart-shaped, thinner and softer than American or Belgian waffles, and traditionally served with sour cream and jam (often a tart berry jam like lingonberry or strawberry) — a beloved snack for any occasion, sweet or savory toppings both welcome. A heart-shaped waffle iron gives the authentic shape, but a regular waffle iron works fine too. Serve warm with sour cream and jam for a sweet option, or with smoked salmon and a dollop of crème fraîche for something more savory.

Spotlight: Kubba (Iraq)

Kubba are Iraqi dumplings — an outer shell made from bulgur wheat or rice, stuffed with a seasoned meat filling (often including pine nuts or raisins for a touch of sweetness), then either boiled, fried, or baked depending on the regional style. Fried kubba are particularly well-suited to a party — shape the bulgur shell around a ball of seasoned ground meat filling, seal completely, and deep-fry until the outside is crisp and golden brown.

Group J — Argentina, Algeria, Austria, Jordan

Group J Teams
Argentina · Algeria · Austria · Jordan

The defending champions get the feature here, and there's no dish more synonymous with Argentine football culture than this one.

⭐ Featured Dish: Choripán (Argentina)

Choripán — literally "chorizo bread" — is the food of Argentine football. It's sold outside every stadium in the country on match day: grilled chorizo sausage, split open, stuffed into crusty bread, and topped with chimichurri, the bright, herby, garlicky sauce that defines Argentine grilling.

For a home version: grill chorizo sausages until well-charred on the outside and cooked through, then split them lengthwise to maximize surface area. For the chimichurri, finely chop fresh parsley and oregano, combine with minced garlic, red wine vinegar, olive oil, a pinch of chili flakes, salt, and pepper — let it sit for at least 20 minutes for the flavors to meld. Stuff the grilled chorizo into crusty bread rolls and spoon chimichurri generously over the top.

This is the dish for Argentina's matches — eaten by Argentine fans at every match, at home or in the stadium, for exactly this reason.

Spotlight: Merguez (Algeria)

Merguez are spicy North African lamb or beef sausages, seasoned heavily with harissa, cumin, and fennel, giving them a distinctive red color and bold, warming heat. They're a staple at gatherings across Algeria and the wider Maghreb region. Grill or pan-fry until well-browned and cooked through, and serve in flatbread with a simple salad of tomato, cucumber, and onion, plus extra harissa for those who want more heat.

Spotlight: Käsekrainer (Austria)

Käsekrainer is an Austrian sausage studded with chunks of cheese that melt and ooze out as it cooks — a beloved street food and beer garden staple, typically served in a crusty roll with mustard. Grill or pan-fry until the sausage is browned and the cheese inside is molten, then serve immediately in a roll with a generous amount of mustard — the contrast between the savory sausage, melted cheese, and sharp mustard is the whole point.

Spotlight: Knafeh (Jordan)

Knafeh is a beloved Middle Eastern dessert with deep roots across the Levant, including Jordan — a base of shredded pastry (kataifi) layered with sweet cheese, baked until golden, then soaked in a fragrant sugar syrup and topped with crushed pistachios. It's rich, sweet, and a genuinely impressive thing to serve at a gathering. While the traditional version requires specific cheese, a simplified version using mozzarella under the pastry layer, baked and syrup-soaked, captures much of the experience — and gives your spread a sweet finish alongside all the savory options in this guide.

Group K — Portugal, Colombia, DR Congo, Uzbekistan

Group K Teams
Portugal · Colombia · DR Congo · Uzbekistan

Ronaldo's sixth and almost certainly final World Cup makes Portugal the natural feature — and Portuguese pastry culture gives us one of the sweetest entries in this entire guide.

⭐ Featured Dish: Pastéis de Nata (Portugal)

Pastéis de nata are Portugal's most famous export to the world of pastry — small, flaky tart shells filled with a rich egg custard, baked at high heat until the top is caramelized and slightly scorched in places, and the pastry shatters at the first bite. They're traditionally eaten warm, dusted with cinnamon and powdered sugar.

For a home version: line a muffin tin with rounds of puff pastry, pressing them into the cups to form little shells. For the custard, whisk together egg yolks, sugar, milk, cream, and a splash of vanilla, then gently heat until slightly thickened — don't let it boil. Pour the custard into the pastry shells and bake at a very high temperature (240°C/450°F or as high as your oven goes) for 15–20 minutes, until the tops are deeply caramelized with dark spots. Let them cool slightly before serving, dusted with cinnamon.

This is the dish for any Portugal match — a sweet treat that works perfectly alongside all the savory options elsewhere in this guide.

Spotlight: Arepas (Colombia)

Arepas are Colombia's everyday staple — round, flat cornmeal cakes, grilled or fried until crisp on the outside, then split open and filled with anything from cheese to shredded meat to avocado. They're endlessly versatile and genuinely simple to make. Mix pre-cooked cornmeal (masarepa) with water and salt to form a dough, shape into discs, and cook on a hot griddle or pan until browned on both sides. Split open while hot and fill with melted cheese, shredded chicken, or whatever your guests prefer.

Spotlight: Mikate (DR Congo)

Mikate are simple fried dough balls — slightly sweet, fluffy on the inside with a golden crust — popular across Central Africa as a breakfast food or snack. They're made from a basic yeasted dough (flour, sugar, yeast, water), left to rise, then spooned into hot oil and fried until puffed and golden. Serve warm, dusted with a little extra sugar if desired — simple, comforting, and easy to make in large batches.

Spotlight: Samsa (Uzbekistan)

Samsa are Uzbekistan's answer to the samosa — flaky baked (rather than fried) pastries filled with seasoned meat (often lamb) and onion, traditionally baked in a tandoor-style oven for a distinctive smoky char on the outside. A home oven version still delivers excellent results: fill rounds or squares of puff pastry with a well-seasoned lamb or beef and onion filling, fold into triangles or rectangles, brush with egg wash, and bake at high heat until deeply golden and crisp.

Group L — England, Croatia, Ghana, Panama

Group L Teams

⭐ Featured Dish: Scotch Eggs (England)

Scotch eggs — a hard or soft-boiled egg wrapped in well-seasoned sausage meat, coated in breadcrumbs, and fried or baked until golden — are a pub classic across England, traditionally eaten cold as a portable snack but just as good warm.

For a home version: boil eggs to your preferred doneness (a slightly soft yolk is traditional and genuinely worth the effort), then peel carefully. Flatten a portion of well-seasoned sausage meat into a thin layer and wrap it completely around each egg, sealing any gaps. Roll each wrapped egg in flour, then beaten egg, then breadcrumbs. Deep-fry until the outside is deeply golden and the sausage meat is cooked through, or bake at a high temperature for a less messy alternative. Serve halved, with English mustard.

This is the dish for England vs Croatia — and for any England match where you want something that can be made ahead and served at room temperature.

Spotlight: Pršut & Cheese Board (Croatia)

Croatia's Dalmatian coast produces some of the best cured meats and cheeses in the Balkans, particularly pršut — a dry-cured ham similar to prosciutto, air-dried for months until intensely flavorful. For a party, the simplest approach is the best one: arrange thinly sliced pršut alongside a hard sheep's cheese (paški sir if you can find it, or a good aged pecorino as a substitute), some olives, and crusty bread. No cooking required — just good ingredients, well presented.

Spotlight: Kelewele (Ghana)

Kelewele is Ghana's beloved spiced fried plantain — ripe plantain cubes marinated in a blend of ginger, chili, and warming spices like nutmeg and cloves before being fried until caramelized and slightly crisp at the edges. The spice marinade is what sets it apart from other fried plantain dishes across the continent. Toss diced ripe plantain in a paste of grated ginger, chili powder, and ground nutmeg, let it sit for 15–20 minutes, then fry in hot oil until golden and caramelized.

Spotlight: Carimañolas (Panama)

Carimañolas are Panamanian fritters made from a dough of boiled and mashed yuca (cassava), shaped around a savory filling — typically seasoned shredded beef or chicken — then formed into a torpedo shape and deep-fried until golden. The yuca dough gives them a distinctive chewy texture different from typical fried snacks. Boil and mash yuca until smooth, form a thin layer around a portion of seasoned shredded meat, shape into an oval, and fry until deeply golden on all sides.
