How to Watch the World Cup at Work Without Getting Fired

✍️ Sasha Denver 📅 June 2026 ⏱ 10 min read

The World Cup is 104 matches across 39 days. Kickoff times range from 12pm to 9pm local time in the host cities — which means a significant portion of the tournament lands squarely in the middle of the working day for fans across North America, and in the evening for the UK and Europe (with the occasional nightmarish 2am BST slot for good measure).

The reality: unless your employer has declared the entire summer a compulsory football holiday — and a few enlightened ones have come close — you are going to be at your desk for some of these matches. This guide is about navigating that situation with your employment, your dignity, and your sanity more or less intact.

Some of what follows is practical and legitimate. Some of it is in the spirit of the tournament. All of it assumes you're a professional who will still hit your deadlines. This isn't advice to stop working — it's advice on how to work and watch, without making it everyone else's problem.

Before anything else: Know which of your team's matches fall during working hours. Use the match times tool — pick your team and get every kickoff in your local timezone. You can't plan around matches you haven't checked.

1. The Browser Tab Strategy

The browser tab is the foundation of all in-office football watching. Master it and most situations become manageable. Neglect it and you'll be explaining to a passing manager why your screen is showing a full-screen stadium in Guadalajara.

The Alt+Tab Reflex

First: muscle memory. Alt+Tab (Windows) or Cmd+Tab (Mac) switches to your last-used application in under a second. Before the match starts, have a work document open and ready as your immediate Alt+Tab destination. Not just minimised — actually open, with some relevant content visible. The switch needs to look natural, not like someone who just panicked and opened a blank Word document.

Practice this until it's instinctive. You want the Alt+Tab to happen before you've consciously processed that someone is walking toward your desk — which means it needs to be a conditioned response, not a deliberate one. The same way you'd naturally turn down music when a call comes in. It takes about an afternoon to become automatic.

The Browser Profile Separation

If your work browser and personal browser are the same application, fix that now. Use Chrome or Firefox profiles, or simply use a different browser for each. Your work tabs (email, the report you're pretending to write, your company's project management tool) live in one profile. Your match stream lives in the other. The benefit isn't just visual separation — it means you won't accidentally share your screen in a meeting and reveal a browser tab helpfully labelled "BBC Sport — LIVE: France 2-0 England."

The Decoy Tab Order

Tab order matters. Keep your work tabs first. The stream should be somewhere in the middle — not the first thing visible if someone glances at your browser. On a wide monitor, a narrow sidebar view of a live score ticker (BBC Sport, Google search "World Cup score", or similar) is often enough to follow the match without a full video stream taking up screen real estate. Live text commentary requires no audio, takes up minimal space, and can be dismissed in a fraction of a second.

The two-monitor advantage: If you have a dual-monitor setup, the geometry is obvious — work on the primary monitor that faces the room, stream on the secondary monitor angled away from the walkway. This is the closest thing to a legitimate watching setup in an open-plan office. Position it carefully and nobody sees anything they weren't meant to.

Windowed, Not Full Screen

Full screen is the cardinal sin. A windowed stream — resized to roughly a quarter of your screen, tucked into a corner — is far less visible from a distance and takes less than a second to minimise or move behind another window. Never, under any circumstances, go full screen during work hours. This rule has no exceptions. A penalty shootout does not constitute an exception.

Browser Setup — Before Kick-Off
  • Work document open and visible as your instant Alt+Tab destination
  • Stream in a separate browser profile or different browser entirely
  • Stream windowed at roughly quarter-screen — never full screen
  • Decoy tabs (email, spreadsheet, report) positioned first in tab order
  • Live score ticker as a lightweight alternative for the lowest-risk periods
  • Screen angle checked — nothing visible from the walkway behind you

2. Audio Without Getting Caught

Audio is where most in-office football watching operations collapse. The visual side can be managed with a bit of discipline. Audio is trickier — because unlike a screen you can minimise, you cannot minimise commentary that your colleagues can hear. The rules here are stricter.

One Earbud, One Ear Out

Two earbuds in is the first mistake. It signals to everyone around you that you're fully elsewhere — it's also a genuine professional social cue that you're unavailable, which tends to invite the people who want to make a point of interrupting. One earbud, at low volume, in the ear angled away from the main foot-traffic direction, is the approach. You can still hear your surroundings. You catch a colleague approaching before they're reading your screen. And if challenged, "just listening to something quietly" is a much easier conversation than "I've been completely absent for forty-five minutes."

Volume Discipline

The volume level that feels private to you is almost certainly audible to the person two desks over. Set it lower than feels necessary. If you can clearly hear the commentary, it's too loud. Aim for "I can just about follow what's happening" rather than "full match experience." If a goal is scored and the crowd roars in your earbud at normal volume, you will react. Your face will give it away even if no sound escapes. That reaction is information. Keep the volume low enough that the surprise of a goal doesn't produce an involuntary celebration.

The Goal Celebration Problem

This is the real hazard. Not the watching — the reacting. A goal, a red card, a penalty miss — these things produce physical responses that are very difficult to suppress, especially when you've been building tension for ninety minutes alone at your desk. The professional move is to prepare for this in advance. Have a story ready. "Just got some good news on a thing" covers a surprisingly wide range of involuntary celebrations. Practice the neutral reaction. Failing that — go to the bathroom at the seventy-fifth minute of a tense match. If something happens while you're not at your desk, you're not responsible for reacting to it.

No Commentary: The Live Ticker Approach

The cleanest audio solution is to use no audio at all. A live text commentary feed — BBC Sport's live text, the Google "World Cup score" result, or a score app on your phone placed face-down on the desk — gives you the key information with zero audio risk. You'll know the score, who scored, how many minutes played, and whether there's a VAR review. You won't get the atmosphere, but you'll have dramatically reduced your operational risk. For group-stage matches where the outcome matters but the experience is secondary, this is often the right call.

"The goal celebration is the tell. Not the watching — the reacting. Control the reaction and you control the situation."

3. Mobile Data vs Office WiFi

This is the most important technical decision you'll make about in-office watching, and the answer is less ambiguous than you might hope. Do not stream on office WiFi. Not as a precaution. Not as a preference. As a rule.

Why IT Can See Everything on Office WiFi

Corporate networks log traffic. Not necessarily in real time with someone watching a dashboard — but the logs exist, and they're reviewed. Every connection your device makes on the company WiFi goes through infrastructure that the IT department controls. Your streaming service, the broadcaster's CDN, the amount of data transferred, the duration — all of it creates a record. Most of the time, nobody looks. But if there's ever a reason to look — a redundancy process, a performance review, a complaint — that record is there. You streamed a 90-minute match on company WiFi at 11am on a Tuesday. It's not a great look.

The Personal Hotspot Setup

The correct approach: watch on your personal device (phone, tablet, or personal laptop) using your phone's mobile data, either directly or via a personal hotspot. Your personal device on your personal data connection goes through your mobile carrier's network, not your employer's. IT does not have visibility into your carrier's traffic. The risk profile is completely different.

There are some practical considerations. Streaming a 90-minute match at standard quality uses roughly 1.5–2GB of mobile data. A 4K stream is significantly more. Check your data allowance before the tournament starts — and if you're watching regularly, consider whether a higher-tier plan is worth the cost for the duration of the group stage. For most people watching occasionally, the data cost is manageable. For someone planning to follow their team through the knockouts from their desk, budgeting the data in advance is worth doing.

If You Must Use Office WiFi

Sometimes the situation doesn't allow for mobile data — you're tethered to a wired connection, the mobile signal is poor in your building, or you're travelling and the data costs are prohibitive. If you find yourself in this position, the next section on VPNs is particularly relevant. A VPN encrypts your traffic so that even on the company network, the destination of your data isn't visible to IT in plain text. It doesn't make you invisible — network admins can still see that data is flowing, and the volume of a live stream is distinctive — but it removes the immediate readability of exactly what you're accessing.

The cleanest setup: Personal device, personal hotspot, VPN running. This is entirely outside your employer's network and your employer's visibility. What you do on your own device on your own data connection is your business.

4. Scheduling Meetings Around Kickoffs

The proactive approach — and the one that requires the least subterfuge — is to shape your calendar before the tournament starts rather than scramble to protect time during it. Most kickoff times are fixed well in advance. The group stage schedule is published. You know when your team plays. There is no excuse for not planning around it.

The "Deep Work" Block Strategy

Block the kickoff window as a calendar event before anyone else claims the slot. The event title matters. "Deep work — no meetings" or "Focus block — [project name]" is the standard corporate language for uninterruptible time, and most colleagues and managers respect it without asking what specifically you're focused on. You're not lying — you will be deeply focused. On a football match.

Book it a week in advance when possible, and book it for slightly longer than the match — kickoff plus two hours covers normal time, with buffer for stoppage time and the possibility of it going to extra time. A blocked calendar slot that you voluntarily open early (because the match ended in normal time) makes you look more responsive, not less committed.

Check the Kickoff Times Now

The 2026 World Cup has three kickoff windows in the host cities: 12pm, 3pm, and 6pm local time (ET). That translates to different windows depending on your timezone — and the difference between a 3pm ET kick-off and a 6pm ET kick-off is significant for scheduling purposes. Use the match times tool to get every one of your team's fixtures in your local time, then put them directly in your calendar. Do this before the tournament starts, not the morning of each match.

Calendar Strategy — Before the Tournament
  • Check all your team's kickoff times using the match times tool
  • Block each kickoff window + 2 hours as a "deep work" or "focus" slot
  • Move any existing meetings at least one week before the tournament starts
  • If you're a meeting organiser, front-load your scheduling to mornings
  • Set your status to "Do Not Disturb" or "In Focus" during match windows

The Art of the Polite Delay

A meeting request lands in your match window despite your best efforts. You have two moves: propose a different time, or accept and manage the exit. "Does 10am work better? I have a hard stop at 3pm" is a sentence that exists in normal professional life and requires no explanation. Use it freely. Most people do not question "I have a hard stop" — it's the socially accepted corporate signifier for "I have somewhere else to be and I'm not going to tell you where."

If you're in a meeting that's running long into your kickoff window, the "hard stop" exit is legitimate. Stand up. "I'm sorry, I have a hard stop — I'll follow up by email on the remaining items." Walk out. Do not explain. Do not apologise twice. A hard stop is a hard stop.

Front-Load Your Productivity

The most effective scheduling strategy is also the simplest: do more productive work in the morning so that the afternoon genuinely feels earned. If you've cleared your inbox, made progress on the important thing, and proactively communicated to your team — the case for having a quiet afternoon is much stronger. This is less about deception and more about actual time management. It also means that if you do get pulled into an unexpected meeting during the match window, you haven't lost a full day of output.

5. The Legitimate Remote Work Play

Everything above involves a degree of managing the tension between watching and working. This section is about eliminating that tension entirely — by not being in the office at all.

WFH Days Are the Strongest Move in the Playbook

Working from home during key matches isn't a compromise. It's the optimal configuration. You're at your desk, you're reachable, you're producing work — and a second monitor, a personal laptop, or even just a phone propped up on the desk lets you follow the match with no performance anxiety about who's walking past. The audio situation improves dramatically. Nobody can see your screen. You can mute yourself and unmute when needed without anyone noticing a 45-minute gap in your contribution.

If your company has any flexibility on WFH days, plan to use them strategically during the tournament. Your team's three group games, any knockout matches, and the semi-finals and Final should be on your personal calendar months in advance. Book WFH days to coincide with as many of those as possible. Do it now. Do it casually — "I'll be WFH that week" is a normal sentence. "I'd like to be WFH specifically on these five days because my team plays at 3pm" is information you do not need to share.

The Productivity Argument Is Actually Valid

There's a reasonable case to be made — to yourself, and potentially to a sympathetic manager — that a focused employee watching a match from home is more productive across the full day than that same employee spending the same hours in the office, distracted, managing the operational complexity of covert in-office viewing. The commute is saved. The cognitive load of "don't get caught" is removed. The afternoon energy is better because the tension of the morning has been released rather than suppressed.

Some managers get this. It's worth finding out if yours is one of them before spending the group stage in a state of low-level anxiety. "I tend to be more productive on WFH days during big sporting events — I'm fully online but I can follow the match without it disrupting the office" is a sentence that a reasonable manager might appreciate more than you expect. The worst that happens is they say no. You're no worse off.

Flexi Time and Make-Up Hours

If your company has a flexi-time arrangement — and many do, without it being formally stated as such — the implicit deal is that the time gets made up. A 90-minute match in the afternoon, followed by 90 minutes of actual work in the evening or the next morning, is a zero-sum trade. You're not taking time off work. You're resequencing when that time happens. Most employers who trust their people don't count hours to the minute. Be the kind of employee who delivers the work and the time resequencing takes care of itself.

"Working from home during your team's match is not a hack. It's the most honest version of this. You're reachable, you're working, and you're not making it anyone else's problem."

6. The VPN Angle

A VPN — Virtual Private Network — is best known as the tool for unlocking geo-restricted streams abroad. But it has a directly relevant function for in-office watching that's worth understanding: it encrypts your internet traffic so that the content of your connections isn't readable in plain text on the corporate network.

Without a VPN on office WiFi, your network traffic can show IT exactly which domains you're connecting to — bbc.co.uk, fox.com, peacocktv.com — which makes it fairly straightforward to identify that you were streaming a live broadcast for 92 minutes on a Tuesday afternoon. With a VPN, that traffic routes through an encrypted tunnel. The destination isn't visible in the logs in the same way.

This isn't a magic shield, and it's not an invitation to behave as if you're invisible on a network you don't own. But for the scenario where you're watching on a personal device and the office WiFi is the only connectivity available, a VPN is a meaningful privacy layer. It's also — and this is the framing that requires no justification to anyone — just good practice for using any public or semi-public WiFi network, regardless of what you're doing on it.

7. Nuclear Options

Sometimes the match is too important for half-measures. Your team is in the quarter-finals. The kickoff is 3pm on a Wednesday. All the browser-tab discipline in the world isn't going to make this feel manageable. In these situations, the only honest move is to not be at work at all. There are several ways to achieve this.

Annual Leave: The Cleanest Nuclear Option

Annual leave is the legitimate, above-board, no-explanation-required version of this. You are entitled to it. Using it to watch a World Cup quarter-final is exactly as valid a use of your leave as using it for any other personal reason — which is to say, completely valid, because it's your leave and the reason is nobody's business. Book it in advance. Do not mention the football. "I've got a few days' leave booked" is a complete sentence.

The strategic error people make is booking leave reactively — waiting until their team reaches a certain stage before committing. By then, other people have already claimed the days, and the conversation with your manager now requires explaining why you suddenly need the 7th of July off, which is conspicuously the day of the semifinal. Book leave for any day your team could potentially be playing a knockout match, from the Round of 32 onward. You can always cancel it if you go out early.

The Tactical Half-Day

Half a day's leave for a 3pm kickoff is a rational transaction. You work the morning, deliver everything you needed to deliver, take half a day's leave from noon, and watch the match at home in peace. This is so normal that it barely qualifies as a nuclear option — but it's worth stating explicitly because a lot of people don't think of it as available to them. Half days exist. Use them. "I'm going to take a half day from midday" is a completely ordinary sentence in most workplaces.

The "Dentist Appointment" (With a Wink)

Dental health is important and unpredictable. Dental appointments can be surprisingly hard to reschedule. This is a well-understood part of working life and one of the more durable pieces of professional folklore around sporting events. We're not recommending it. We're simply acknowledging its existence as a cultural institution. Your conscience is your own business.

The Sick Day: A Cost-Benefit Reminder

Sick days exist for when you're genuinely unwell. Using one dishonestly comes with costs that are easy to underestimate in the moment: your team's results don't track with your actual health, patterns become visible over time, and the guilt of a wasted sick day on a match your team loses tends to compound the general misery. This is included not as a recommendation, but as a reminder that the economics of it are worse than they first appear.

The final principle: Before you use any leave, check whether the match is actually worth it. Use the match times tool to confirm your team's kickoff time in your local timezone — and then ask yourself honestly whether this is a Group C third-place decider or a semi-final. Calibrate accordingly. Annual leave is finite and semi-finals are rare.

Is Your Match Worth It?

Here is the honest framework: nuclear options — leave, half days, creative scheduling — are appropriate for your team's knockout matches from the Round of 16 onward. The group stage, unless it's a genuine six-pointer or your team's opening match, is browser-tab territory. Save your leave for the matches where losing would genuinely ruin your week regardless of whether you watched live or caught the highlights at 6pm. Use it strategically. The tournament runs 39 days. You cannot nuke every significant match. Pick your moments and commit to them properly.
